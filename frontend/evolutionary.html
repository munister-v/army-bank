01 · The Problem

Banks operate inside a search space they cannot enumerate.

Modern banking decisions are not single-variable choices — they are high-dimensional, multi-objective, often non-convex problems. Classical optimization breaks down precisely where it matters most.

2⁵⁰
Possible portfolio allocations

For 50 candidate loans with binary include/exclude, the search space is ≈ 1.1 × 10¹⁵ — beyond brute-force.

≥ 4
Conflicting objectives

Maximize expected return, minimize variance, satisfy capital ratios, preserve liquidity buffers. No single optimum exists.

∇f ≠ ∇f
Non-differentiable surface

Regulatory constraints introduce discontinuities. Gradient methods stall at local optima or fail to converge entirely.

“The hardest problems in finance are not those where the optimum is hidden — they are those where the optimum is one of millions, all nearly equally good, and none of them obvious.”

Where classical methods fail

Method Assumption Failure mode
Linear programming Linear objective & constraints Risk & default models are non-linear
Gradient descent Differentiable, convex landscape Stuck in local optima; no gradient at constraints
Branch-and-bound Bounded combinatorial space Exponential explosion past ~30 dimensions
Monte Carlo search No structure exploitable No memory — wastes evaluations on redundant regions
02 · Mathematical Foundation

Evolution as an optimization operator.

Evolutionary computation reframes optimization as a stochastic search guided by selection pressure. A population of candidate solutions iteratively improves through three operators inspired by natural genetics.

// Formal definition Population: Pₜ = { x₁, x₂, …, xₙ } ∈ Ωⁿ Fitness: f: Ω → ℝ Goal: x* = argmaxₓ∈Ω f(x) // Generation transition Pₜ₊₁ = Mutate( Crossover( Select(Pₜ, f) ) ) // Selection probability (proportional / roulette) Pr(xᵢ) = f(xᵢ) / Σⱼ f(xⱼ)

The three operators

1

Selection

Choose parents with probability proportional to their fitness. Fitter individuals reproduce more — directional pressure.

2

Crossover

Combine genetic material of two parents to form offspring. Exploits structure already discovered in the population.

3

Mutation

Apply small random perturbations. Introduces novelty — preserves exploration and escapes local optima.

Why it works — Schema Theorem

Holland (1975) proved that short, low-order schemata with above-average fitness receive exponentially increasing trials in subsequent generations:

m(H, t+1) ≥ m(H, t) · f(H)/f̄ · [1 − pc · δ(H)/(L−1) − o(H) · pm]

Where m(H,t) is schema count at generation t, f(H) its fitness, δ(H) its defining length and o(H) its order. Implication: good building blocks compound.

03 · Algorithmic Walk-through

Anatomy of a single generation.

1

Initialize population

Generate n random candidate solutions across the feasible region. Diversity at t = 0 determines reachable optima.

2

Evaluate fitness

Compute f(xᵢ) for each individual. In banking: simulate the portfolio's expected return under a stochastic risk model.

3

Selection (tournament k = 3)

Draw three random individuals; the fittest becomes a parent. Repeat until n parents are chosen. Preserves diversity better than roulette.

4

Crossover (uniform, pc = 0.85)

For each pair of parents, swap each gene independently with 50% probability. Produces offspring inheriting traits from both.

5

Mutation (Gaussian, σ = 0.05)

With small probability, perturb each gene by 𝒩(0, σ²). Maintains exploration; prevents premature convergence.

6

Replacement (elitism, e = 2)

Carry the top-2 individuals into the next generation untouched. Guarantees monotonic improvement of the best-so-far.

7

Repeat until convergence

Stop when best fitness stagnates for T generations or a wall-clock budget expires. Return the elite individual as x*.

04 · Application at ARM Bank

Credit-portfolio optimization, in production.

ARM Bank issues consumer and SME credit lines daily. Each new application competes for a limited capital budget under regulatory risk constraints. We frame this as a multi-objective optimization problem solved by an evolutionary engine.

Decision variables

For each candidate loan i in the daily queue:

xᵢ ∈ [0, 1] // fraction of requested amount approved

A 32-loan day → ℝ32 continuous search space (≈ 10⁹⁶ effective resolutions at 8-bit precision).

Multi-objective fitness

f(x) = w₁·E[R(x)] − w₂·σ(x) − w₃·VaR₉₅(x) − λ·max(0, ∑xᵢ·aᵢ − B)²

E[R] expected return · σ volatility · VaR95 95-percentile loss · B capital budget · λ penalty.

Constraints encoded as soft penalties

Basel III

Risk-weighted assets < 12× Tier-1 capital.

Liquidity

Maintain ≥ 18% of deposits as on-demand reserve.

Concentration

No sector > 25% of total exposure.

05 · System Architecture

How the engine plugs into the bank.

The evolutionary optimizer runs as an isolated micro-service. It consumes the daily application queue, the current capital state and a market snapshot — and emits an approval vector ready for the credit officer to ratify.

Convergence behaviour

Across 50 independent restarts, the engine reaches 99% of best-known fitness within 80 generations on average. The population diversity (Hamming distance to centroid) decays smoothly without collapsing before generation 30 — the empirical signature of a healthy exploration-exploitation balance.

08 · Conclusion

A general-purpose lever for hard banking decisions.

Evolutionary computation is not a single algorithm — it is a paradigm. Wherever the bank faces a decision space too large to enumerate and too irregular for calculus, the same engine, re-skinned with a new fitness function, applies.

Immediate next applications

  • ATM cash-replenishment scheduling
  • Branch staffing & opening-hours assignment
  • Marketing-campaign segment selection
  • Fraud-detection feature engineering (genetic programming)
  • Treasury hedging-instrument mix

Why now

Modern CPUs evaluate 200-individual populations in milliseconds. The cost of a generation is a rounding error against the cost of a single mis-priced loan. The mathematics has been settled for fifty years. The bottleneck was always the will to deploy — and the scaffolding to interpret the output. Both are now in place at ARM Bank.

“Evolution is the only optimization algorithm whose efficiency we have actually measured against the universe — and it is the one that built every working system you have ever encountered.”
